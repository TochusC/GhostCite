Review #1144A
===========================================================================

Impact & Reasons to accept
--------------------------
This paper tackles a problem of growing relevance: that of fabricated LLM-generated references in scientific papers. The paper contributes an open source infrastructure to identify hallucinated references, an analysis of the frequency of hallucinated references across models and research domains, an analysis of papers at ML/security venues with hallucinated references, and a survey with researchers to understand practices towards using LLMs for assistance with finding references in papers as authors and for checking reference veracity as reviewers. To that end, his paper clearly represents a lot of work.

I am impressed by the manual verification of references flagged as invalid by CiteVerifier as well as the false negative spot checking.

I also really liked the detailed case studies of citations flagged as invalid in 6.1 to help understand common failure modes.

Limitations to add
------------------
If accepted, the paper should acknowledge these limitations:

- The external validity of Experiment I is a limitation that should be discussed. The setup, where citations are generated by asking for blocks of N, may not accurately reflect real-world user behavior, which limits the generalizability of the results and should be explicitly acknowledged.

- The human-subjects survey methodology is limited by the inclusion of potentially leading and abstract questions (as noted with Table 13). This methodological choice may introduce biases (such as priming and social desirability bias), affecting the rigor of the data.

Constructive suggestions to improve the paper
---------------------------------------------
- The abstract and introduction are both quite long and could pack greater punch by reducing detail and highlighting key points that align with key contributions.

- The paper does not make a strong and direct relevance claim for security specifically. The big relevance to security is simply that the big 4 security venues are included in the corpus. If accepted, the paper should strengthen this argument — I think it is not too difficult to make the claim that ghost citations are a new form of computing abuse that must be identified, measured, and mitigate to preserve the integrity of the scientific ecosystem.

- Quite frankly, for the “For researchers” paragraph in 8.2, I would go further: researchers should not be citing papers they have not at least read the abstracts of. They should not be citing even real papers that have been retrieved by AI tools without actually reading at least a summary of the work directly from the source.

Reasons to not accept the paper
-------------------------------
For Experiment I, it is unclear to me how representative the set-up is to real-world use and thus the external validity of the results. It seems unlikely that people generate citations by asking specifically for blocks of N citations; at the very least, this should be grounded. Accordingly, Experiment 1 may have internal validity across models but little external validity. Unfortunately, the results are presented as being external valid (or at least this is not acknowledged).

Looking at the human-subjects survey in Table 13, there are a number of leading and abstract questions that go against best practices for survey design (see Redmiles, E. M., Acar, Y., Fahl, S., & Mazurek, M. L. (2017). A summary of survey methodology best practices for security and privacy researchers). I expect there is a lot of priming and social desirability bias in the data as a result. This piece of the work does not quite meet the rigor bar that would be expected at a conference like SEC.

Questions for the author response
---------------------------------
Is there data / empirical work that shows how authors use LLMs to generate citations for their work? If not, how might you increase the external validity of Experiment 1 or tone down claims?

How might you strengthen the relevance of this work to the security community beyond simply including security papers in the corpus?

Review #1144B
===========================================================================

Impact & Reasons to accept
--------------------------
- *Timely investigation of AI tool reliability in research workflows:* The paper addresses the risk of hallucinated citations from AI-assisted citation generation, which is increasingly relevant as these tools are adopted in academic settings.
- *Mixed-methods study design:* Combining a measurement study with a survey of researchers allows the work to capture both empirical performance and human perceptions/practices, increasing the richness of the results.
- *Manual verification of results:* Manual validation of tool outputs strengthens the credibility of findings.

Limitations to add
------------------
- *Experimental design limitations:* The selection of representative papers across domains is not fully justified, which may limit generalizability and affect interpretability of results.
- *Focus on titles only for tool limits tool utility:* Having the tool only evaluate titles limits it's generalizabilty of the tool as it focuses exclusively on citations of non-existent papers rather than citations that are incorrect
- *Discuss social desirability and bias:* Address potential biases in survey responses and consider implications for interpreting the data. This is especially relevant given the nature of what the survey is asking about

Constructive suggestions to improve the paper
---------------------------------------------
- *Clarify security relevance:* Explicitly articulate how hallucinated citations impact security research, including implications for literature reviews, threat modeling, or replication of security studies.
- *Refine experimental design:* Focus more narrowly on the security domain, potentially dividing S&P papers into subdomains and evaluating AI tools on these subsets. This could provide more meaningful, domain-specific insights. I would instead focus only on S&P as the domain and pull papers for each subdomain in the space, possibly identified by various tracks at conferences. Then I'd ask the LLM to produce related papers to each subdomain, further broken into subsubdomains and do comparisons across that as well. This would yield richer insights and have more relevance to USENIX security. 
- *Detail survey analysis:* Clearly describe how survey responses were processed, including coding for qualitative answers and any statistical tests used for quantitative responses.
- *Reconcile tool vs. manual evaluation:* Ensure that analyses of LLM outputs are consistent with the tool’s design (title-based evaluation) or clarify differences.

Reasons to not accept the paper
-------------------------------
- *Unclear S&P relevance:* The paper does not fully justify its connection to security research or USENIX Security specifically, which weakens the case for acceptance. The analysis of Ghost Cites is done for many broad CS domains rather than focusing on S&P. The survey is done with S&P PC members and AI PC members. This limits the applicability of these results to a S&P venue. 
- *Experimental design limitations:* The selection and evaluation of representative papers may not accurately reflect realistic researcher workflows, limiting generalizability. The prompt asks LLMs to generate related papers for domains as broad as security and cryptography based on a representative paper. For example, cryptography and security is a huge domain so how was one paper selected? I'd also imagine that depending on how niche the area is within that domain, it could impact the number of hallucinations as there could be more or fewer relevant papers to actually return so if the minimum number is too high to match the domain, this would also impact results. I think this part of the experiment needs further thought, both to be relevant to USENIX Security and to yield better results. I think my major concern is the design of the study does not reflect how real researchers would use these tools and thus does not provide broad generalization or high impact.
- *Incomplete methodological detail:* Survey analysis and tool evaluation methodology are insufficiently described, limiting confidence in the conclusions.
- *Presentation issues:* Figures (Figure 1 and Figure 3) and visualizations are difficult to interpret, reducing clarity of the results.

Questions for the author response
---------------------------------
- How were the representative papers determined for the LLM experiment?
- Why focus on so many domains rather than just S&P?
